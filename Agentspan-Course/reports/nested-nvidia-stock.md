# NVIDIA Stock: What the Latest Results Mean

NVIDIA remains one of the market’s most closely watched stocks because it sits at the center of the AI infrastructure buildout. Recent filings and earnings releases reinforce the main reason investors have been bullish: growth remains extraordinary, especially in Data Center. But those same disclosures also highlight the stock’s risks, particularly around export controls, customer concentration, and supply-chain dependence.

## Growth is still the core of the NVIDIA story

The headline numbers remain exceptional. NVIDIA reported fiscal 2025 revenue of **$130.5 billion**, up **114% year over year**. In the fourth quarter of fiscal 2025, revenue reached **$39.3 billion**, up **12% sequentially** and **78% from a year earlier**.

Data Center continues to drive the business. In Q4 fiscal 2025, Data Center revenue was **$35.6 billion**, up **16% sequentially** and **93% year over year**. That matters because it confirms NVIDIA is increasingly tied to AI training and inference demand, not just its older gaming-centered identity.

The momentum carried into the next quarter. In Q1 fiscal 2026, NVIDIA reported revenue of **$44.1 billion**, up **12% from Q4 fiscal 2025** and **69% from the prior-year quarter**. Data Center revenue rose to **$39.1 billion**, up **10% sequentially** and **73% year over year**.

Put simply, NVIDIA is still growing rapidly from an already massive base—something few companies at this scale can do.

## Profitability is strong, but policy risk can hit margins quickly

For fiscal 2025, NVIDIA reported a **GAAP gross margin of 75.0%** and **GAAP diluted EPS of $2.94**. Those are unusually strong profitability figures and help explain why investors have been willing to give the stock a premium valuation.

Still, the latest quarter showed how quickly outside shocks can affect results. In Q1 fiscal 2026, GAAP gross margin fell to **60.5%**, while non-GAAP gross margin was **61.0%**. NVIDIA said non-GAAP gross margin would have been **71.3% excluding the H20 charge**, suggesting a major one-time policy impact rather than a broad deterioration in operating performance.

That impact was significant. NVIDIA disclosed a **$4.5 billion charge in Q1 FY2026** tied to **H20 excess inventory and purchase obligations** after new export license requirements for China. The company also said it **was unable to ship an additional $2.5 billion of H20 revenue** in the quarter because of those restrictions.

For investors, the takeaway is clear: demand may remain strong, but regulation can still sharply affect revenue timing, product mix, and margins.

## NVIDIA is no longer just a chip company

NVIDIA describes itself as a platform company spanning **Data Center, Gaming, Professional Visualization, Automotive, and OEM/Other**. That framing matters. The company is positioning itself not simply as a GPU vendor, but as a broader computing platform provider.

Its competitive advantage rests on the combination of **GPUs, networking, systems, and software**, especially its **CUDA** and **CUDA-X** ecosystem. This software layer is a major reason NVIDIA has maintained such a strong position in AI. It creates switching costs, shortens development cycles, and makes the hardware more valuable than raw chip performance alone would suggest.

Management has also emphasized its next-generation product cycle. In the Q4 fiscal 2025 release, NVIDIA said **Blackwell achieved billions of dollars in sales in its first quarter**. In the Q1 fiscal 2026 release, management said **Blackwell NVL72 is in full-scale production across system makers and cloud service providers**. That suggests the product cycle is advancing despite export-related disruption.

## The business model is powerful, but dependent on partners

NVIDIA is a **fabless** semiconductor company. It designs chips and systems but depends on external partners for manufacturing. That model has helped the company scale efficiently, but it also creates meaningful dependencies.

The annual report says NVIDIA relies on foundry, assembly, test, and packaging partners. It also notes **limited supply sources for many key components and services**, meaning shortages or delays can directly affect its ability to meet demand.

That is especially important in advanced AI hardware, where **advanced packaging, substrates, memory, and manufacturing capacity** can become bottlenecks. NVIDIA specifically warns that constraints in these areas could limit growth.

There is also geographic concentration risk. Much of the semiconductor supply chain is centered in Asia, and NVIDIA says geopolitical tensions, natural disasters, or disruptions involving **Taiwan and the region** could materially affect operations. Because NVIDIA depends on companies such as **TSMC** and other upstream suppliers, execution is not driven by demand alone.

## Customer concentration remains a real risk

NVIDIA’s growth has been extraordinary, but it is not fully diversified. The company disclosed that in **fiscal 2025, two direct customers each represented 10% or more of total revenue**, contributing **13%** and **11%**, respectively. In **fiscal 2024, one direct customer represented 13% of total revenue**.

Those figures matter because they show how much revenue can depend on a relatively small number of large buyers. The annual report adds an important nuance: a substantial portion of revenue comes through a limited number of partners and distributors, meaning direct-customer concentration may understate the concentration of actual end demand.

NVIDIA also says indirect customer revenue is estimated using purchase-order information and channel data, so visibility into final customer concentration is imperfect. In practice, that means a small number of large cloud or enterprise buyers may have an outsized influence on results, even if that is not fully visible in reported disclosures.

## China and export controls remain one of the biggest overhangs

Export-control risk is now one of the clearest issues in the NVIDIA investment case. The company said that on **April 9, 2025**, it was informed that a **license is required for exports of H20 products into China**. The annual report also states that export controls and related restrictions have already affected, and may continue to affect, the business.

This goes beyond a single quarter’s charge. NVIDIA warns that further export restrictions could reduce demand, hinder product development, and benefit competitors in affected markets. The company also disclosed that regulators in China are investigating whether compliance with U.S. export controls discriminates unfairly against Chinese customers, adding another layer of uncertainty.

For a company operating in a strategically sensitive industry, geopolitical developments can directly affect sales, product strategy, and long-term competitive positioning.

## The investment case: major upside, but not low risk

The bull case for NVIDIA stock remains straightforward. The company is delivering **very high revenue growth**, Data Center remains a powerful engine, and its software ecosystem and full-stack AI infrastructure approach strengthen its competitive position. If AI spending continues to expand at scale, NVIDIA is still one of the clearest beneficiaries.

The bear case is also well supported by the company’s own disclosures. NVIDIA faces meaningful exposure to **customer concentration**, **export-control disruptions**, **manufacturing and supply-chain dependence**, and **reliance on a small number of products and regions**. The Q1 fiscal 2026 H20 charge showed that even with strong core demand, a policy change can have a major financial impact very quickly.

## Bottom line

NVIDIA remains one of the strongest operating stories in the market. Revenue growth, Data Center momentum, and ecosystem strength all support the view that it remains a central AI infrastructure provider. But the stock is not simply a bet on AI demand. It is also a bet that NVIDIA can navigate geopolitical restrictions, supply bottlenecks, and concentrated customer exposure without a lasting hit to growth or profitability.

That mix of elite execution and elevated external risk is what makes NVIDIA stock so compelling—and so difficult to value.
